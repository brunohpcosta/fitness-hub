# 13–22 August — the ten days to the 42 km

Built for one goal: arrive at the start line on Saturday 22 August as fresh as
possible, having rehearsed everything that can be rehearsed. Every
recommendation is sourced, and where the evidence is practitioner consensus
rather than primary literature that is said plainly.

---

## 1. The principle everything follows from

**No training in the next ten days can make you fitter for race day.**

Adaptations to a session take roughly 10–14 days to express. Anything you do
from today onward arrives too late to help, but is early enough to hurt. The
entire remaining job is to shed fatigue without shedding fitness, and to test
the things that can still be tested — fuelling, pacing, kit.

This is not a reason to do nothing. It is a reason to change what training is
*for*.

---

## 2. What the taper literature actually says

The reference point is a meta-analysis of 27 studies:

> The optimal strategy is a tapering intervention of **2-week duration** with
> training volume **exponentially decreased by 41–60%**, **without any
> modification of either training intensity or frequency**.
>
> — Bosquet, Montpetit, Arvisais & Mujika (2007), *Med Sci Sports Exerc*
> 39(8):1358–1367

Three things fall out of that, and they are the design rules below:

**Cut volume, hard.** 41–60% is a large reduction. Your recent running is
around 26 km/week; race week should land near 12 km.

**Do not cut intensity.** Removing all fast running during a taper is a common
mistake. A short race-pace block stays in.

**Do not cut frequency.** Keep running three times a week. Fewer, longer
sessions are the wrong shape.

For strength the picture is equally clear. A 14-day detraining period in power
athletes left maximal concentric and isometric strength unchanged. Ten days of
reduced lifting costs you nothing measurable. You are not going to lose six
years of work in a week and a half.

## 3. The 15 August rehearsal — I am revising the distance

PROJECT-STATE has this at 28–30 km. **I would run 26–28 km, at the lower end if
anything feels off.** Here is the reasoning, including where I think the earlier
justification was wrong.

The stated case for going long was injury-risk reduction: a longer run keeps a
bigger number inside the 30-day window and shrinks the race-day distance spike.
Checking that against the bands already in your knowledge table:

| Longest in window | Spike to 42.195 km | BJSM band |
|---|---|---|
| 23.02 km (as things stand) | +83% | >30–100%, HRR 1.52 |
| 26 km | +62% | >30–100%, HRR 1.52 |
| 28 km | +51% | >30–100%, HRR 1.52 |
| 30 km | +41% | >30–100%, HRR 1.52 |

**Every option lands in the same band.** Reaching the next band down would need
a run over 32 km, which is not happening seven days out. So the injury-risk
argument for pushing to 30 km does not survive contact with the numbers, and
both PROJECT-STATE and the fuelling plan overstated it.

Meanwhile the costs of going longer are real:

- **The rehearsal is itself a spike.** 30 km against your 23.02 km longest is
  +30%; 26 km is +13%. Going long means two large single-session increases
  inside eight days rather than one.
- **Seven days is tight recovery.** Standard practice puts the last long run
  2–3 weeks out precisely because recovery from a near-race-length effort takes
  longer than a week. Yours is already a compromise at seven days.
- **You have a knee history.** That argues for the smaller of two defensible
  options, not the larger.

And the benefit that actually matters is fully available at 26 km. At 6:05/km
that is 2h38 — long enough to take eight gels, to find out how your gut handles
65 g/h under race intensity, and to practise the pacing discipline. A further
four kilometres tests nothing new.

**Caveats, stated rather than buried.** The BJSM bands are observational, use
self-reported injury outcomes, and are non-monotonic — the >10–30% band carries
a *higher* point estimate than >30–100%, which should make anyone cautious about
reading them too precisely. "Same band" means the evidence does not support
claiming a difference, not that the risks are identical.

## 5. The gym work

**The last lower-body loading was Wednesday 12 August. There is no more.**

Practitioner guidance on when to stop heavy lifting ranges from three days to
two weeks out; there is no strong consensus. Given a knee history, a race
distance well beyond anything you have run, and nothing to gain from further
loading, the conservative end is the right end.

Rules for the two remaining sessions:

- **Upper body only.** No squats, deadlifts, leg press, lunges, calf work.
- **No eccentric emphasis.** Slow negatives cause the most muscle damage and
  the most delayed soreness. Controlled but not deliberately slow.
- **No new exercises.** Nothing untested goes in during race week, ever.
- **No plyometrics, no jumping, no heavy carries.**
- **Cut sets, keep load.** Same weights you would normally use, roughly half
  the sets. This is what "reduce volume, maintain intensity" means in the gym.
- **No glute isolation** — as established previously, it is redundant given
  your compound strength and running volume.

## 6. The 15 August rehearsal, in detail

Treat it as a dress rehearsal, not a training run. It is the only test you get.

- **Distance:** 26–28 km
- **Pace:** 6:00–6:10/km for the bulk, **last 5 km at 5:41/km**
- **Fuel: 65 g/h — a gel every 20 minutes**, exactly as on race day. At 2h38
  that is 8 serves
- **Same gels, same variants, same caffeine split** you intend to race with
- **150–250 ml of water with every gel**
- **Same shoes, same socks, same shorts.** Nothing new on the 22nd
- **Start deliberately slow.** The first 3 km should feel too easy

Log what you took, when, and how your gut felt at 30, 60, 90 and 120 minutes.

**If 65 g/h causes problems, drop to 55–60 for race day.** Better a slightly
under-fuelled finish than a stop at 30 km. There is no time for a second
experiment.

---

## 7. What this plan does not fix

The plan reduces avoidable risk. It does not remove the central one.

You are attempting 42.195 km with a longest recent run of 23 km, and no taper
design changes that. The last 10 km will be the hardest thing in the run and
pace is not the limiter — endurance base is. Going out slower than feels right
is worth more on the day than anything in this document.

If your knee gives you pain that changes your gait, stop. That is not a taper
question, and no finish time is worth it.
